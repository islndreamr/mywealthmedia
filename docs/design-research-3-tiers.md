# Why the site still feels lacking: three tiers of research, one diagnosis

Nov 12, 2026. Three separate research tracks ran in parallel, then I measured our own preview against what they found. Every claim names its source and its tier. Nothing here is a number I made up. The full raw reports are long; this is the synthesis.

The three tiers
- Tier 1, evidence: measured studies on what makes a consultant's page convert. Eye-tracking labs, usability research, benchmark reports over tens of thousands of pages.
- Tier 2, peers: the real, live homepages of 15 solo experts, fractional COOs and small studios, read page by page.
- Tier 3, craft: award-listed editorial and luxury sites plus the writing of working typographers, to find what makes a page feel designed instead of assembled.

## The diagnosis in one paragraph

The page has every ingredient on the list and makes no large decisions. The designed sites have fewer ingredients and one big move per screen. Ours makes five or six medium moves in every section and repeats the same recipe six times: numeral, heading, lede, grid of equal boxes, button. The research on first impressions says people judge the page in 50 milliseconds, before reading, and that simple, familiar, low-clutter pages rate highest. Our first screen has 14 separate elements, a headline that fills 8 percent of the screen height, a decorated panel, a masked photo, two calls to action and an eyebrow label. It is busy without being bold. Below the fold, the thing the peer sites put second (a named client result with a number) is four screens down on ours and has no number yet.

## What each tier found, and what it means for us

### Tier 1: the evidence

Confirmed by primary research:
- People form a stable judgment of a page's appeal within 50 ms, before reading (Lindgaard et al., 2006, Behaviour and Information Technology).
- 57 percent of viewing time goes above the fold and 74 percent to the first two screenfuls; most of that lands in the top half of the first screen (Nielsen Norman Group, Scrolling and Attention, 2018).
- Leaving risk is highest in the first 10 seconds; the value proposition must land inside that window (NN/g, 2011, from Liu, White and Dumais, 2010).
- Low visual complexity plus a familiar layout gets the highest appeal ratings, and complexity matters far more than colour (Tuch et al., 2012, Google Research; Reinecke et al., CHI 2013).
- Eye-tracking shows people look at photos of real people as content and ignore stock and decorative images (NN/g, Photos as Web Content, 2010).
- Numerals attract fixations even inside text that scanners skip (NN/g, Show Numbers as Numerals, 2007).
- Credibility comes from design quality, up-front disclosure, verifiable claims and connection to the outside world (NN/g, Trustworthiness in Web Design, 2016; Fogg, Stanford Web Credibility, 2002).
- Button colour is not proven to matter; contrast with surroundings is (CXL analysis of vendor tests). "Red converts best" is a myth.
- Moving elements pull attention away from the goal; carousels get ignored like ads (NN/g, 2013).

Benchmark, vendor data: Unbounce's 2024 report puts the professional-services median at about 6.1 percent and the top quarter at about 14 percent, on campaign pages hosted by Unbounce. Treat as a rough yardstick, not a target.

Not proven anywhere research-grade, despite how often they are repeated: that the photo should look toward the button, that sticky bars always help, that named testimonials convert X percent better, that first person beats third person. These are hypotheses, not rules.

What it means for us: the first screen is nearly the whole game, and ours is cluttered. Our proof strip uses numerals correctly but it is about the salon years, not client outcomes. Our trust signals are thin because the client results have no numbers and no names (the no-names part is your choice; the numbers are the gap).

### Tier 2: the peers

The strongest five for our purpose were Simon Wakeman (solo fractional COO), The COO Solution, ScaleUpExec, Novaa Studio and Jay Clouse, with Justin Welsh as the model of restraint. What they share:
- The headline names the buyer's situation, not the seller's title. "Fractional COO services for founders who are done being the bottleneck." "The founder's COO."
- Proof arrives on the first or second screen: a named result with a number ("from $65K to $350K per month"), a logo row, a rating, or a peer quote.
- One call-to-action phrase, repeated word for word in nav, hero, after the process and in a floating mobile bar.
- The booking block says what the call is and is not: 30 minutes, no pitch, no commitment, "if your path is already clear, we will tell you that too." Two of the five embed the calendar on the homepage.
- Fit stated in numbers: revenue range, team size, business type.
- One line of friction or opinion per page: "not a consultant with theory, untested frameworks and a lot of slides." "They take direct reports."
- A smaller step under the booking form for people not ready: a quiz, a worksheet, a free mockup.
- One portrait, large, looking at the lens or toward the headline. No stock, no illustration.

The weaker sites share a generic headline, anonymous praise without a result, counters starting from zero, and a nav full of services.

What it means for us: our headline is a promise, which is fine, but nothing on the first screen says who it is for in numbers. Our CTA wording is already consistent, which is good. Our booking page has no "no pitch" copy and no calendar yet. We have no fit numbers (that is a placeholder in the FAQ). We have no opinion line anywhere. Our proof is out of order.

### Tier 3: the craft

From award-listed sites (Elena Scott, Heart and Soil, TR Studio, Speakeasy, Every, Grace Walker, Natalya Nechaeva, Noah Stokes, Eli Horn, Philip Readman), luxury and magazine sites (Kinfolk, The Gentlewoman, The Row, Le Labo, Byredo) and practitioner writing (Frank Chimero, Jen Simmons, Andy Clarke's Smashing Magazine series, NN/g's Good Visual Design, Richard Rutter, Rob Weychert, Ellen Lupton). The ten craft moves:

1. One idea per screen. TR Studio's first screen is a colour and a paragraph.
2. Make one thing oversized. Nechaeva's name is about a tenth of the screen wide. Lupton: small size differences look "tentative and arbitrary."
3. Then use only three type sizes (NN/g). We run seven or eight.
4. Empty space is the luxury material. Clarke gives a quarter of a layout to white space "to give this design a feeling of luxury."
5. Asymmetric columns. Twelve equal columns is "tapping twelve beats on your desk" (Clarke). Use 2|1|1|2, or leave a skinny column empty.
6. Let things overlap or bleed. Nechaeva's portrait sits over her name. Nothing on our page touches anything.
7. Photo continuity. Heart and Soil's photo backdrop matches the panel beside it. Our arch mask seals the photo off instead. The arch is the single most "template" signal on the page: a decoration applied to the subject rather than a decision about the subject.
8. Navigation as a few words, not a system. Two to five words. A rail of six numbered steps tells the reader the page is a form.
9. Vary the module. Six sections of the same height and the same "0N Heading" structure have one tempo. One full-width module per page slows the reader down.
10. Repeat a signature, not a template. Every repeats swash capitals. Le Labo repeats material plus number.

On the accent colour: the designed sites use it once per screen. Pink appears on our page in six buttons plus bullet dots, step markers and the quiz border. At that frequency it stops being an accent and becomes interface chrome.

The tells of an assembled layout, verbatim from the craft report: equal columns of three; every section the same height; everything inside a card with the same radius; every element the same distance apart; nothing overlapping; nothing cropped; nothing oversized; an eyebrow above every heading; the accent on every button; a stat strip; the same hero recipe. We hit nearly all of them.

## What I recommend, in order

Group A: the first screen. Biggest payoff, no new copy needed.
1. Headline at roughly 8 to 10 percent of screen width, three lines, the only large thing on the screen. Drop the eyebrow and the subline from the hero; the subline moves to a standfirst below the fold.
2. Replace the arch with a full-height rectangular portrait that runs off the right edge of the screen, with the headline's last line overlapping its left edge. The photograph becomes the dark, warm area; the decorated ink panel goes away.
3. One pink button. "Get the free guide" becomes a plain text link.
4. One proof sentence under the button, in numerals.

Group B: the order of the page. Needs the result numbers from you.
5. Second block on the page becomes one client result as a large italic pull quote with the number as the biggest thing in it. The salon proof strip moves down into the story.
6. "Who I work with" block with fit in numbers (revenue range, team size, business type). This is the FAQ placeholder you have not filled yet; it belongs on the page, not in the FAQ.

Group C: craft. No copy changes.
7. Type scale cut to three sizes plus one small label size.
8. Services out of cards: three unequal text columns with a hairline above and no borders. Client results out of cards: pull quotes.
9. Pink once per screen. Every other button becomes an outlined ink pill.
10. A 2|1|1|2 grid with an empty narrow column, and one full-width module (your story) as the slow moment. Section heights vary on purpose.
11. Rail reduced to four words with no numerals. The numerals move into the content as one oversized numeral per screen where a count means something. This changes the brief, so it is your call.

Group D: voice and the booking block. Needs your words.
12. Booking block copy: 30 minutes, with you, no pitch, no commitment, "you leave with clear next steps whether we work together or not." Calendar embedded on the page when the link arrives. The free guide becomes the "not ready to talk?" fallback under it.
13. One line of friction per page, in your voice: what you will not do. "Not a deck." "Not a plan you file." Your words, not mine.
14. Every client result captured specifically and in the client's words, with one number each.

Groups A and C are about a day of work. Group B waits on three numbers. Group D waits on a few sentences from you.
